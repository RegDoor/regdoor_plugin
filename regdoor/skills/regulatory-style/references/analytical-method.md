# Analytical method

## Build the issue map

Before drafting, determine:

1. the object of analysis;
2. whether it is an entity, activity, instrument, infrastructure, legal regime, market practice, or proposal;
3. the relevant actors;
4. the jurisdiction or jurisdictions;
5. the relevant date or period;
6. the current legal or procedural status;
7. the material evidence and missing facts;
8. the unresolved regulatory or operational hurdle; and
9. the practical consequence.

Do not expose private reasoning. Include only assumptions, limitations, or analytical steps needed for the reader to evaluate the answer.

## Escalate analytical depth only when needed

Escalate to provision-level work when a reader could reach a different legal or operational conclusion depending on an article, paragraph, condition, exception, alternative, or commencement rule. Read [legal-regulatory-quality.md](legal-regulatory-quality.md) at that level.

Analytical depth determines the evidence and legal reasoning needed for a reliable conclusion. The delivery register determines how that conclusion is ordered and expressed. Select them independently: accessible newsletter prose does not justify omitting a material condition, source, qualification, or consequence that would be required in a regulatory report. Adapt the presentation through [output-patterns.md](output-patterns.md), not by reducing the underlying analysis.

## Classify before concluding

Separate, when relevant:

- the instrument, product, or technology;
- the entity providing, issuing, operating, or controlling it;
- the regulated activity;
- the user-facing use case;
- the infrastructure or intermediary involved; and
- the jurisdictional perimeter.

Do not treat a technology provider, issuer, intermediary, custodian, broker, exchange, payment institution, market infrastructure, and protocol as interchangeable.

Use a taxonomy only when it changes the conclusion. Use inline `(i)`, `(ii)`, `(iii)`, and at most `(iv)` for conceptual decomposition that belongs in one paragraph. Use bullets or a table for longer or operational lists.

For provision-level work, make the legal modality explicit when it matters: distinguish an obligation, prohibition, permission or faculty, exception, condition, procedural requirement, consequence, and supervisory power. Do not collapse them into a generic statement that the instrument “provides” or “establishes” something.

## Determine status precisely

Distinguish among:

- in force;
- adopted but pending implementation;
- proposed or introduced;
- under consultation;
- guidance or interpretation;
- supervisory expectation;
- sandbox, pilot, or experiment;
- market practice;
- private company statement;
- transitional or grandfathered; and
- expired, withdrawn, stayed, or unknown.

Identify the procedural step that supports the status. Do not infer binding effect from a title, announcement, speech, consultation, or market report.

Distinguish adoption, publication, commencement, implementation, transition, and present enforceability. A final instrument with deferred commencement is neither a proposal nor a presently operative obligation.

## Explain mechanism and consequence

Do not say that a rule creates clarity, improves efficiency, or supports innovation without explaining how. Identify the relevant change in authorization, disclosure, custody, reserves, settlement, reporting, liability, ownership, redemption, tax, data access, interoperability, enforcement, cost, timing, or risk.

Translate the mechanism into an actor-specific consequence. State what an issuer, intermediary, infrastructure operator, regulated institution, service provider, investor, user, or supervisor must do, assess, monitor, disclose, restructure, or avoid.

Use a `Misconception` or `Hurdle` block only when it corrects a real market narrative or isolates a genuinely unresolved condition. Do not manufacture one to fill a template.

## Apply the informational-value test

After drafting, test each sentence and contextual paragraph:

1. **Material role:** identify whether it adds nature, scope, mechanism, actor, condition, status, consequence, uncertainty, or evidence.
2. **Deletion:** remove or integrate it if the legal meaning and reader's decision remain unchanged.
3. **Negation:** retain a negative formulation only if a reasonable reader could otherwise confuse categories, effects, modalities, or legal status. Do not carry it over automatically from the source draft.
4. **Material timing:** apply the entrypoint's timing-materiality rule; do not treat a complete publication-to-enforceability chronology as a mandatory inventory.
5. **Context budget:** describe the base instrument, history, and surrounding controls only to the extent needed to understand the principal change and its consequence. If only named provisions are extended, state those provisions rather than inventorying the rest of the regime positively or negatively.
6. **Preservation gate:** confirm that compression has not removed a material condition, exception, definition, source, provision locator, legal qualification, acronym explanation, or actor-specific consequence.

Prefer a selective explanation of the affected part of a base regime to a catalog of everything that regime regulates. An inventory is justified only when its individual elements change implementation, risk, or the requested decision.

For a regulatory update, inspect the first substantive paragraph separately. It must expose the concrete new mechanism, not merely announce that an instrument amends or expands another regime.

For a rewrite, derive a material-proposition inventory before editing: principal change, operative mechanism, actors, triggers, conditions, exceptions, timing, consequences, sources, and qualifications. Preserve that inventory, not the original sentence sequence. Omit examples, broad control catalogs, contrastive framing, duplicate act dates, gazette publication dates, and cutoff-based status narration unless the specific detail changes scope, validity, applicability, temporal comparison, compliance, or the requested decision. Preserve a commencement or compliance date when it does.

## Compare regulatory architectures

Compare who and what each system regulates, which authority has jurisdiction, whether authorization, passporting, equivalence, recognition, or exemptions exist, the status of each framework, and the operational consequence.

Do not reduce the comparison to a list of statutes. Explain why the same business model, actor, or transaction may trigger different obligations across jurisdictions.

## Preserve uncertainty

Use calibrated language when evidence is incomplete. Distinguish:

- a source-confirmed fact;
- a reasonable inference from identified facts;
- an interpretation that may be contested; and
- a conclusion that cannot be reached without additional facts or authority.

Do not add a disclaimer mechanically. State the specific limitation that affects the conclusion.
